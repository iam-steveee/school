How do Organisms Reproduce?

A complete, readable walkthrough of reproduction: why it happens, how DNA copying creates continuity and variation, asexual reproduction, sexual reproduction in flowering plants, human reproduction, puberty and reproductive health.

01
Foundation

Why do organisms reproduce?

Reproduction is the biological process by which organisms produce new individuals of their own kind. An individual organism can survive without reproducing, but reproduction is essential for the continuity of a species.

Continuity

New individuals replace organisms that die and keep the species going from one generation to the next.

Heredity

Genetic information in DNA is passed from parents to offspring, so offspring resemble their parents.

Variation

Small differences can appear when DNA is copied. These differences can become important when conditions change.

Important distinction: reproduction is not needed for the survival of one individual, but it is needed for the survival and continuation of the species.
02
Genetic continuity

DNA copying and variation

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) carries hereditary information. In a cell, DNA is associated with proteins and is organised as chromatin. During cell division, the chromatin becomes more condensed into chromosomes. Genes are functional units of hereditary information located on DNA.

What happens before a cell divides?

  1. The cell grows and prepares for division.
  2. Its DNA is copied so that there are two copies of the genetic information.
  3. The copies are distributed as the cell divides.
  4. Each daughter cell receives genetic information.

Why are offspring not always exact copies?

DNA copying is generally accurate, but biological copying can involve small differences. Environmental factors and genetic processes can also contribute to variation. In asexual reproduction, these variations are usually small because there is only one parent. In sexual reproduction, genetic material comes from two parents, so combinations of traits create more variation.

Exam idea: Variation is not automatically “good” or “bad”. A variation that helps survival in one environment may be less useful in another.
03
One parent

Asexual reproduction

Asexual reproduction involves a single parent and does not involve the fusion of male and female gametes. It is usually rapid and produces offspring that are genetically very similar to the parent, although small variations can arise.

03.1 Binary fission

In binary fission, a unicellular organism divides into two daughter cells. The genetic material is copied and the cell then divides. In Amoeba, division can occur in different planes. In organisms such as Leishmania, division occurs in a definite orientation related to its body structure.

Amoeba

Division can occur in almost any plane because of its flexible body.

Leishmania

Division has a definite orientation associated with its elongated body and flagellum.

Paramecium

Binary fission occurs across a particular plane of the body.

03.2 Multiple fission

In multiple fission, the nucleus divides repeatedly first. Cytoplasm then surrounds the nuclei, producing many daughter cells. Plasmodium, the malarial parasite, is a standard example.

03.3 Fragmentation

In fragmentation, a multicellular organism breaks into pieces and each suitable fragment grows into a new individual. Spirogyra is a common example. It works because its simple body does not have complex organs that must remain together.

03.4 Regeneration

Regeneration is the ability of certain organisms to regrow lost body parts. In some simple organisms, specialised cells can divide and differentiate so that a fragment can develop into a complete individual. Planaria and Hydra are commonly used examples in school biology.

Planaria / body fragment→cell division→differentiation→new structures

03.5 Budding

In budding, a small outgrowth called a bud develops on the parent body. The bud grows by cell division and may detach to live independently. Hydra and Yeast are standard examples.

03.6 Spore formation

Rhizopus, the bread mould, forms spores inside structures called sporangia. Spores have protective walls and can survive unfavourable conditions. When they reach a suitable moist environment, they germinate and grow into new organisms.

04
Asexual reproduction in plants

Vegetative propagation

Vegetative propagation is a form of asexual reproduction in which new plants develop from vegetative parts such as roots, stems or leaves rather than from seeds.

PartExampleHow it works
StemPotato, sugarcaneBuds/nodes develop into new shoots.
RootSweet potato, DahliaModified roots can produce new shoots.
LeafBryophyllumBuds along leaf margins develop into plantlets.

Natural and artificial methods

Cutting

A piece of stem is planted and develops roots and shoots. Rose and hibiscus are common examples.

Layering

A branch is bent to the ground and covered with soil until roots develop. Jasmine is a common example.

Grafting

A shoot or branch from one plant is joined to another rooted plant so the tissues grow together.

Tissue culture

A small amount of plant tissue is grown on a nutrient medium under controlled conditions to produce many plants.

Why use vegetative propagation?

It is useful when a plant does not produce viable seeds, when seed production is slow, or when a grower wants to preserve desirable characteristics. Because offspring are genetically very similar to the parent, useful traits can be maintained.

Its limitation is equally important: low genetic variation means a disease or environmental change that harms the parent type may also affect many of its clones.

05
Two gametes

Sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction involves the formation of male and female gametes and their fusion during fertilisation. Because genetic material is combined from two sources, offspring generally show more variation than offspring produced by asexual reproduction.

Gamete

A reproductive cell that carries genetic material and participates in fertilisation.

Fertilisation

Fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote.

Zygote

The first cell formed after fertilisation; it divides and develops into the new organism.

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

A flower is the reproductive structure of a flowering plant. The stamen is the male reproductive part and the pistil/carpel is the female reproductive part.

PartFunction
SepalsProtect the flower when it is in the bud stage.
PetalsOften attract pollinators through colour, shape or scent.
AntherProduces pollen grains.
FilamentSupports the anther.
StigmaReceives pollen; its surface is suited to pollen capture.
StyleConnects stigma and ovary; the pollen tube grows through it.
OvaryContains ovules and later develops into the fruit after fertilisation.
OvuleContains the female gamete and develops into a seed after fertilisation.

Unisexual vs bisexual flowers

A unisexual flower has either stamens or carpels. A bisexual flower has both stamens and carpels in the same flower. Papaya and watermelon are common examples of plants with unisexual flowers; hibiscus and mustard have bisexual flowers.

Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. It occurs before fertilisation.

Self-pollination

Pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. It usually produces less genetic variation.

Cross-pollination

Pollen moves between flowers on different plants of the same species. Wind, water and animals can act as agents and it generally promotes more variation.

Fertilisation

  1. A pollen grain lands on a compatible stigma.
  2. The pollen grain germinates and forms a pollen tube.
  3. The pollen tube grows through the style towards an ovule.
  4. The male gamete reaches the egg cell.
  5. The male and female gametes fuse to form a zygote.

What changes after fertilisation?

Zygote → embryo

The zygote divides and develops into an embryo.

Ovule → seed

The fertilised ovule develops into a seed, usually with a protective seed coat.

Ovary → fruit

The ovary develops into a fruit that surrounds or contains the seeds.

Seed and germination

A seed contains an embryo and stored food. The radicle develops into the root system and the plumule develops into the shoot system. Germination begins when a viable seed gets suitable conditions such as water, oxygen and an appropriate temperature.

06
Human reproduction

Puberty, gametes and reproductive systems

Human reproduction involves specialised reproductive organs and gametes. Puberty is the period when hormonal changes cause the reproductive system to mature and the body develops secondary sexual characteristics.

Hormones

Hormones coordinate many changes of puberty and help reproductive organs mature.

Common changes

Growth spurt, changes in body shape, development of body hair, skin changes and maturation of reproductive organs.

Individual variation

The timing and pattern of puberty vary from person to person. Variation is normal.

Male gamete: sperm

Sperm are male gametes produced in the testes. They are small cells specialised for movement and for carrying genetic material to the female gamete.

Female gamete: ovum

An ovum (egg) is the female gamete produced in the ovaries. It is larger than a sperm cell and contains genetic material and cytoplasm that supports the early stages after fertilisation.

Male reproductive system

StructureMain role
TestesProduce sperm and the hormone testosterone.
ScrotumHolds the testes outside the main body cavity and helps maintain a suitable temperature for sperm production.
Vas deferensCarries sperm away from the testes.
Seminal vesicles & prostateAdd fluids that contribute to semen and support sperm movement.
UrethraPassage through which semen leaves the body; it also carries urine at different times.

Female reproductive system

StructureMain role
OvariesProduce ova and reproductive hormones.
Oviducts / fallopian tubesCarry the ovum towards the uterus; fertilisation commonly occurs here.
UterusMuscular organ where an embryo can implant and develop.
CervixLower part of the uterus connecting to the vagina.
VaginaMuscular canal leading from the cervix to the outside of the body.

Fertilisation, implantation and development

During fertilisation, a sperm and egg fuse to form a zygote. The zygote divides repeatedly as it moves towards the uterus. If it successfully attaches to the uterine lining, the process is called implantation. The developing embryo receives nutrients and oxygen through the placenta.

Menstruation and the menstrual cycle

Each menstrual cycle involves changes in the ovary and uterus. The uterine lining becomes thicker in preparation for a possible pregnancy. If fertilisation and implantation do not occur, the thickened lining is shed as menstruation.

Remember: menstruation is not a disease. It is a normal biological process associated with the reproductive cycle.
07
Health & responsibility

Reproductive health

Reproductive health means maintaining physical, emotional and social well-being in matters related to the reproductive system. It includes accurate knowledge, hygiene, prevention of infections, access to healthcare and responsible decision-making.

Sexually transmitted infections

Some infections can be transmitted through sexual contact. Examples taught at this level include gonorrhoea, syphilis and HIV infection/AIDS. Prevention depends on accurate information, safer practices, testing where appropriate and medical care.

Important: a person should never be mocked or blamed for having an infection. Many infections can be treated or managed, and medical advice matters.

Contraception

Contraceptive methods are used to reduce the chance of pregnancy. Different methods work in different ways, and no single method is appropriate for every person.

Barrier methods

Methods such as condoms create a physical barrier that reduces the chance of sperm reaching the egg. Condoms also reduce the risk of many sexually transmitted infections.

Hormonal methods

Hormonal contraceptives can prevent ovulation or alter reproductive conditions. They require appropriate medical guidance.

IUCDs

Intrauterine contraceptive devices are placed in the uterus by trained healthcare professionals.

Surgical methods

Vasectomy and tubectomy are permanent methods that block the pathways used by sperm or eggs.

For a Class 10 learner, the key idea is to understand the purpose and broad working principle of these methods, not to memorise medical instructions.

Questions you should be able to answer after studying

  • Why is reproduction important for a species but not necessary for an individual organism's survival?
  • How does DNA copying connect reproduction with heredity and variation?
  • How are binary fission and multiple fission different?
  • Why can simple organisms regenerate while complex organisms generally cannot form a complete organism from every fragment?
  • How does vegetative propagation differ from reproduction through seeds?
  • What are the functions of the main parts of a flower?
  • Differentiate self-pollination and cross-pollination.
  • Explain the sequence from pollination to fertilisation to seed and fruit formation.

  • Why does menstruation occur when fertilisation does not lead to pregnancy?
