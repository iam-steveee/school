01 · FOUNDATION

Why reproduce?

Continuity · heredity · variation

Reproduction is the biological process through which organisms produce new individuals. It is essential for the continuity of a species, because individual organisms eventually die but reproduction allows the species to continue.

Reproduction also transfers hereditary information through DNA. During reproduction, DNA is copied and passed to the next generation. The copies are usually very similar, but small differences can arise. These differences are called variations.

CORE IDEAReproduction ≠ growthGrowth makes an individual larger or more developed. Reproduction produces new individuals.
EXAM LINKWhy is variation important?Variation can help a population survive environmental changes and provides the raw material for evolution.

02 · GENETIC CONTINUITY

DNA copying & variation

DNA contains the information used to build and maintain an organism. Before a cell divides, its DNA is copied. Each resulting cell receives a copy of the genetic material.

DNA copying is not perfectly error-free. Small changes can therefore appear. In asexual reproduction, these differences are usually small, while sexual reproduction generates greater variation because genetic material from two parents is combined.

DNAHereditary material carrying genetic information.
ChromatinDNA associated with proteins in the nucleus.
ChromosomeCondensed chromatin visible during cell division.

03 · ONE PARENT

Asexual reproduction

Fast · simple · usually low variation

Asexual reproduction involves a single parent. There is no fusion of male and female gametes. Because the offspring arise from one parent, they are usually genetically very similar to that parent.

It is common in organisms with simple body organisation and can be a rapid way of increasing numbers when conditions are favourable.

ASEXUAL1 parentNo gamete fusion
SEXUAL2 gametesFusion → zygote
03.1

Binary fission

A unicellular parent divides into two daughter cells. Amoeba can divide in different planes; organisms such as Leishmania show a definite orientation.

03.2

Multiple fission

The nucleus divides repeatedly and the cytoplasm later forms many daughter cells. Plasmodium is the standard Class 10 example.

03.3

Fragmentation

A simple multicellular organism such as Spirogyra can break into fragments; each fragment grows into a new individual under suitable conditions.

03.4

Regeneration

Specialised cells can proliferate and differentiate so that a body fragment develops into a complete organism. Planaria is the classic example.

03.5

Budding

A small outgrowth, or bud, develops on the parent and grows into a new individual. Hydra and yeast are common examples.

03.6

Spore formation

Rhizopus produces spores inside sporangia. The spores have protective walls and can germinate when they reach suitable conditions.

04 · PLANTS

Vegetative propagation

New plants can arise from vegetative parts such as roots, stems and leaves, without seed formation. This is a form of asexual reproduction.

StemPotato eyes, ginger and similar structures.
LeafBuds along the leaf margins of Bryophyllum.
RootSweet potato and dahlia are examples.

Artificial methods include cutting, layering, grafting and tissue culture. Tissue culture can rapidly produce many plants from small amounts of plant tissue under controlled conditions.

ADVANTAGES
  • Rapid production of plants.
  • Parent characteristics are preserved.
  • Useful for plants that produce few or no viable seeds.
  • Many plants can be produced efficiently by tissue culture.
LIMITATIONS
  • Low genetic variation means a shared disease or environmental change can affect many plants similarly.
  • Diseases present in parent material can sometimes be carried to new plants.

05 · TWO GAMETES

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

Flower → pollination → fertilisation → seed

A flower is the reproductive structure of a flowering plant. The stamen is the male reproductive part and the pistil/carpel is the female reproductive part.

PistilStigma + style + ovary. The ovary contains ovules.

POLLINATION TYPES

Self vs cross-pollination

Self-pollination transfers pollen to the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination transfers pollen from one plant to another plant of the same species.

SELFSame plantUsually less new variation.
CROSSDifferent plantPromotes more variation.

POST-FERTILISATION

From zygote to seed

Fertilisation produces a zygote. The zygote develops into an embryo. The ovule becomes a seed and the ovary develops into a fruit. Under suitable conditions, the seed germinates and produces a new plant.

Remember: Pollination is the transfer of pollen. Fertilisation is the fusion of gametes. They are not the same process.

06 · HUMANS

Human reproduction

Puberty · gametes · reproductive systems

Puberty is the period during which the reproductive organs mature and the body undergoes hormonal and physical changes. These changes prepare the body for reproductive maturity.

Male and female gametes are specialised cells. The male gamete is the sperm; the female gamete is the ovum/egg.

MALE GAMETESpermSmall and motile; produced in the testes.
FEMALE GAMETEOvum / eggLarger and non-motile; produced in the ovaries.

MALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

Where sperm are produced and transported

The testes produce sperm and testosterone. The scrotum helps maintain a suitable temperature for sperm production. Sperm travel through ducts including the vas deferens, and glands add fluids that form semen.

FEMALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

Ovary → oviduct → uterus

The ovaries produce ova and hormones. The fallopian tubes/oviducts carry the egg towards the uterus and are the usual site of fertilisation. The uterus is where the embryo implants and develops.

The cervix connects the uterus with the vagina.

FERTILISATION & IMPLANTATION

What happens after fertilisation?

Fertilisation occurs when a sperm fuses with the egg to form a zygote. The zygote divides repeatedly, becomes an embryo and moves towards the uterus. Implantation is the attachment of the developing embryo to the uterine lining.

PLACENTA

A specialised exchange organ

The placenta provides a surface through which nutrients and oxygen can pass from the mother's blood to the developing embryo, while carbon dioxide and other wastes move in the opposite direction.

07 · REPRODUCTIVE HEALTH

Health, cycles & responsible choices

Puberty · menstruation · STIs · contraception

PUBERTY

Changes during adolescence

Puberty includes physical and hormonal changes. Common changes include growth in body size, development of reproductive organs, growth of hair in new areas, voice changes in boys, and the beginning of menstruation in girls. The timing varies between individuals.

The important Class 10 idea is that puberty marks reproductive maturity, not a requirement to compare one person's development with another's.

MENSTRUATION

Why does menstruation occur?

After ovulation, the uterine lining becomes thicker and more richly supplied with blood in preparation for possible implantation. If fertilisation does not occur, this lining is shed. This shedding is called menstruation.

MenarcheThe first menstrual period.
MenstruationShedding of the uterine lining when pregnancy does not occur.
MenopauseNatural end of menstrual cycles and reproductive phase.
STIs / STDs

Infections matter

Sexually transmitted infections can spread through sexual contact. Examples in the Class 10 chapter include HIV/AIDS, gonorrhoea and syphilis. Awareness, prevention and appropriate medical care are important.

CONTRACEPTION

Different methods

Methods include barrier methods, oral contraceptives, intrauterine devices and surgical methods. Barrier methods such as condoms can also reduce the risk of some sexually transmitted infections.

KEY IDEA

Health is broader than pregnancy

Reproductive health includes physical, emotional and social well-being, awareness, prevention of disease and access to appropriate healthcare.
